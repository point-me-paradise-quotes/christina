A plain word about accessibility in Rome

Europe does not handle accessibility the way the United States does, and Rome's historic center is one of the harder places. There is no equivalent here of the ADA standard that American travelers are used to. Italy has accessibility laws, but buildings that are centuries old are often exempt or only partly adapted, and far fewer accommodations are built in or expected.

  • Entrances and elevators. A step or two at the door is common. Elevators are often small, sometimes fitting two people, and can start a few steps above the lobby.
  • Bathrooms. Bathtubs with high sides are still widespread, grab bars are rare, and a walk in shower is never certain until the room is assigned.
  • The word accessible on a listing. It is reported by the hotel itself and means less than it does at home. It describes a feature that exists somewhere in the building, not in every room.
  • Streets. Expect cobblestones, uneven sidewalks, curbs without ramps and slopes. In the restricted traffic zones a taxi may stop a short walk from the door.
  • Restaurants and sights. Restrooms are often down a flight of stairs, and many churches and ruins have steps with no handrail.
  • The people. Hotel staff are usually kind and will carry a bag or offer an arm, but they cannot change the building, and help is given when asked for more often than it is offered.

None of this should spoil the trip. It means choosing a hotel for its street as much as its rooms, using taxis freely, wearing shoes with a good grip, and allowing more time than the map suggests.

Grand Hotel Palace

Via Veneto glamour, with a poolVia Vittorio Veneto 70 · 5 star · Millennium Hotels · 86 rooms · built 1927Visit the hotel website for photos and rooms ↗

Marcello Piacentini designed the Palace in 1927, across from the American Embassy, and it still carries the mood of Via Veneto in its dolce vita years: frescoed salons, a terrace on the boulevard, and interiors reworked by the designer Italo Rota. Downstairs is something rare in central Rome, an indoor heated saltwater pool with a sauna and hot tub.

Outside the door

Via Veneto is a broad, tree lined boulevard with wide paved sidewalks instead of cobblestones. The hotel sits partway up its slope: about 5 minutes down to Piazza Barberini and uphill on the return. There is valet service, and taxis pull up at the door, which makes the climb optional.

Inside

There is an elevator, described in an older review as compact. Bathrooms have showers only, and guests describe large marble walk in showers. Whether there are steps at the Via Veneto entrance is not published.

Good to know

  • Rome charges a city tax, paid at the hotel at check out: about EUR 10 per person per night at 5 star hotels and about EUR 7.50 at 4 star hotels.
  • Every hold on this page can be cancelled without charge until its decision date. After that date the room is either paid or released.
