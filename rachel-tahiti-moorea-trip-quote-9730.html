The shape of the week

How this trip works

United flies San Francisco to Papeete once a day, and it leaves at 11:55 a.m. Everything else bends around that. You land at Fa'a'ā at 6:40 p.m. on Saturday, sleep on Tahiti, and give yourself three nights on the main island before crossing to Mo'orea for the back half of the week.

That split is deliberate. Tahiti is where the airport is, where the market and the mountains are, and where you shake off the flight. Mo'orea is thirty minutes across the water and is the part you'll remember. Three nights, then four.

The thing that should decide this: United only flies to Papeete once a day. If you miss that 11:55 a.m. departure, the next one is twenty-four hours later. You'd end up spending the night in San Francisco anyway, except you'd be paying for it twice over in a lost night of resort you'd already booked, plus whatever the rebooking costs.

Option B is legal and United sells it, so on paper it works. In practice: an 8:20 a.m. departure means you're at Bush by 6:15 and awake around 4:45. Then you land at 10:42 with seventy-three minutes to get from United's domestic gates to International Terminal G. The walk is airside, so there's no security to re-clear — though gate assignments won't publish for months. A forty-minute ground delay in Houston, which is not rare in February, puts your week at risk.

Option A takes that worry away, and I'll be straight with you about what it costs. You land at 12:04 a.m. and you're not in a room much before 1. You're up again by 9. It's a short night rather than a real one, and it's a hotel night out of pocket on top of the fare.

Where I land: Option A, staying on airport property. The Grand Hyatt at SFO connects to the terminals by AirTrain — no shuttle line at midnight, no rideshare at 9 a.m., and no wrestling suitcases into a van at 1 a.m. It runs more than the highway hotels in Millbrae or Burlingame, and on a night this compressed, with your bags in tow, it earns the difference. I'll price it once you tell me which way you're leaning.

If you're set on Option B, I suggest we consider insurance with missed-connection coverage. I've done this route without issues myself, but you never know with flights these days.

Your bags on Option A — confirmed, and it matters. They do not check through to Papeete on an overnight connection. You collect them at San Francisco baggage claim after midnight, take them to the hotel, and re-check them in the morning. I've flown this routing myself, so this isn't a guess. It's another reason to stay on airport property rather than shuttle to a highway hotel with luggage at one in the morning. On Option B, with a same-day connection, this doesn't come up at all.

Part three · Nights four through seven

Where you stay on Mo'orea

Four nights, February 23 to 27. The crossing is handled — details below. Two resorts on opposite corners of the island, priced from a garden bungalow with its own plunge pool up to a premium overwater.

You don't need to do anything here. I book both crossings, hold your tickets, and put the QR codes in your trip app alongside everything else. You walk on. The approximate figure above covers both of you — tickets run roughly 1,300 to 2,000 XPF each way per adult depending on which operator sails at the time you want — so you can see what it costs rather than wonder.

One note on where this sits in the numbers. The ferry will be included in your trip, but it is not in the air and hotel figures shown on this page yet. It gets folded into your final rate once you've picked your flights and your bungalow, and I'll confirm the exact amount then rather than carry an estimate through to the invoice.

Why the outbound is on the 23rd and not the 20th. There are no overnight crossings, and the last boat leaves in the late afternoon. You land at 6:40 p.m. on the 20th, which puts you at the terminal after the last sailing regardless of how fast customs moves. That's the reason your Tahiti nights exist — the schedule made the decision, not me.

The return has to be planned, not improvised. Your flight home leaves Fa'a'ā at 8:40 p.m. on the 27th. I'll put you on an afternoon boat with real margin, not the last one, so a delayed sailing never becomes a missed flight. Bags are not an issue — luggage handling on this route is relaxed and people bring far more than you will.

One thing worth knowing: Vaiare is an arrival harbor, not a place. Whatever you're doing on Mo'orea is a short drive from it, and I'll have your onward transfer sorted before you're off the boat.

Now the two resorts themselves, honestly

You've seen the rooms and the numbers. This is the part underneath them. Both properties are four-star, both are on the north coast, both have overwater bungalows and a spa — and they are not interchangeable. Open either one: the strengths and the things worth knowing are both there, because you should hear the second list from me rather than find it on arrival.

What's strong
  • The lagoon is the better one. Guests who have stayed at both consistently rate the snorkelling here above Mo'orea's other overwater resorts. The overwater bungalows sit further out over the lagoon rather than close to shore, and the water underneath them is clearer.
  • Bigger rooms, better bathrooms. The garden bungalows run 74 and 87 sq m against the Manava's 44 to 48. Repeat guests comparing the two properties single out the bathrooms here.
  • More resort. Three restaurants and three bars, a lagoon-style pool, a full spa, tennis, and a fitness centre across a larger property.
  • Spread out. The bungalows are distributed over a wide area rather than clustered, which is the practical form of privacy at a resort this size.
  • The setting. Directly in front of Mount Rotui, between Cook's and ʻOpunohu bays, and a ten-minute walk from Ta'ahiamanu Beach.
  • The Polynesian show is genuinely well regarded by people who have seen a lot of them.
What to know
  • You'll want a car. There is very little within walking distance. Guests who didn't rent one describe the property as isolated — dinner off-site becomes a logistics question rather than a decision.
  • The beach is small. Well kept, plenty of chairs and shade, but modest for the size of the resort. This is not a long stretch of sand.
  • Polished, and a touch overdone. Everything is well kept and smoothly run, which is most of why people like it. If you're after something rougher around the edges and more local-feeling, this leans a little more manicured than that. Taste, not a flaw.
  • It's a strong four-star, not a five. People arriving from Bora Bora's top properties should calibrate accordingly.
  • The buffet on show night is expensive and frequently flagged as not worth it. The show is worth staying for; the dinner is optional.
  • Farthest from the ferry of the two, at about 25 minutes.

Who this suits: travellers who want the best water of the two, more space, and a full resort to stay inside — and who are happy to rent a car or eat on property most nights.

Where I'd put you, and why

Short version first: if getting in the water matters to you at all, book the Hilton. I went back and forth on these two, and the snorkelling is the one thing that isn't close. The Hilton's lagoon is clearer, its overwater bungalows sit further out rather than hugging the shore, and you can go in off your own steps and actually see something worth seeing. The Manava's water is deeper and murkier and its bungalows sit closer in on a shelf. Guests who've stayed at both say the same thing, and I'm not going to dress it up for you.

That's the honest headline. Now the part that complicates it.

What the Hilton asks of you is that you settle in and stay put. There's almost nothing within walking distance, so you'll want a car, and dinner becomes a plan rather than a decision. The beach is well kept but small for a resort that size, and it runs a little more manicured than some people want from an island. None of that is a problem if you're picturing four days of pool, lagoon and not much else. It's a real one if the idea of eating on property four nights running makes you tired.

What the Manava gives you back is a village. You walk out the gate to a grocery, a bank and a handful of restaurants people genuinely rate, which changes how the evenings feel. It's smaller and more Polynesian in character, it's ten minutes closer to the ferry, and the view from its overwater bungalows back at those jagged peaks is the best thing about the property. It's also meaningfully cheaper, and every room I've quoted you there has a real twin bed rather than a fold-out. What you're giving up is the water out front, and I'd rather you go in knowing that than find out on your first morning.

So here's how I'd decide it. If your days are going to revolve around the lagoon in front of your bungalow, pay for the Hilton and rent a car. If you're happy to drive twenty minutes west to Tiahura for the good snorkelling, which is better than anything either resort has out front, then the Manava saves you real money and gives you a nicer evening. Neither of these is the wrong answer. They're just different trips.

If you asked me to pick tonight, I'd put you in the Manava beach bungalow and book you a morning out at Tiahura, and I'd put what you saved toward the things you'll actually remember. But if you tell me you want to walk into the water from your own deck and have it be beautiful, I'll move you to the Hilton without a second thought and I won't try to talk you out of it.

Everything I've written above comes from what guests who've stayed at both keep reporting, not from brochures. I left the unflattering parts in on purpose. You should hear them from me now rather than find them out on day one.
