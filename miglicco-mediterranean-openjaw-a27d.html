The Trip

One-way through the Mediterranean

Every sailing here is open jaw — you board in one port and step off in another, hundreds of miles away. That's the whole advantage. A round-trip itinerary has to spend its last days sailing back to where it started; a one-way spends them pushing further east. You see more coastline and repeat nothing.

It does change how the trip gets booked. Your flights are a multi-city ticket rather than a round-trip, and you'll want a night or two at the embarkation port before you sail — not as a luxury, but as insurance. A delayed connection on the day the ship leaves is the one problem nobody can fix from the pier.

Four sailings, listed in date order. Three run Rome to Athens; one reverses it. Two are large Princess ships and two are the same small Windstar yacht sailing the route in opposite directions. Why this ship changes the itinerary

Look at the port list and you'll notice something: you have probably not heard of half of them. Galaxidi. Fiskardo. Gallipoli. Reggio di Calabria. That isn't an accident, and it isn't a compromise — it's the entire reason to sail this ship.

Star Legend carries 312 guests. A large cruise ship carries ten times that, which means it needs deep water, a long quay, and a town with the infrastructure to absorb three thousand people stepping off at nine in the morning. There are perhaps twenty ports in the Mediterranean that qualify, which is why every big ship calls at the same ones. A small ship has no such constraint. It ties up in the middle of Sorrento. It anchors off a fishing village on Kephalonia. It goes to the places that are still, for the most part, just places.

The trade is intimacy for scale. There is no waterslide, no casino floor, no production show with a cast of forty. What there is instead: a stern platform that folds down in calm anchorages so you can swim, kayak, or paddleboard directly off the back of the ship; an open bridge you can walk onto and talk to the officers; no formal nights, no assigned dining time, no table you're expected at. You eat when you're hungry, with whoever you like.

The Corinth Canal

Between the Italian ports and the Greek ones, this sailing transits the Corinth Canal — a channel cut straight through the isthmus in 1893, four miles long, with limestone walls rising more than 200 feet on either side. It is barely 70 feet wide at the waterline. Ships pass through with a matter of feet to spare on each side, which is why almost no cruise ship can do it at all. Go up on deck for this one; it takes about half an hour and there is nothing else like it.

Nine ports in ten nights. This is the fuller of the Mediterranean routings — Italy, Sicily twice, Malta, and then four Greek and Turkish calls before Athens. One day at sea to catch your breath.

Where you go

  • Embark
    Civitavecchia (for Rome), ItalyThe port sits about 90 minutes northwest of central Rome. Come in ahead — two nights in the city is the right amount before you sail.
  • Port
    Amalfi Coast (Salerno), ItalySalerno is the practical door to the Amalfi Drive — Ravello, Amalfi town, Positano strung along the cliff road. The ferry from Salerno beats the coast road in traffic, and by a wide margin.
  • Port
    Sicily (Messina), ItalyMessina is where you go inland or up. Taormina and its Greek theatre are about 45 minutes south; Etna is the other direction and takes most of the day.
  • Port
    Sicily (Palermo), ItalyA second Sicilian day, and a completely different one. Palermo is Norman cathedrals, Arab-Norman mosaics at Monreale, and street food — panelle, arancine, sfincione — eaten standing up at the Ballarò market.
  • Port
    Valletta, MaltaThe ship berths in the Grand Harbour, directly below the bastion walls. The city is small enough to cross on foot: St. John's Co-Cathedral, the Upper Barrakka Gardens, and the Caravaggio hanging where it was painted to hang.
  • Port
    Santorini, GreeceA tender port — you come ashore by boat, then take the cable car, the donkey path, or the switchback road up to Fira. Oia is a bus ride north and worth the trip early rather than at sunset.
  • Port
    Mykonos, GreeceLittle Venice, the windmills above the waterfront, and a warren of whitewashed lanes designed centuries ago to confuse pirates. It still works on visitors.
  • Port
    Kusadasi, Türkiye (for Ephesus)Roughly 30 minutes from the pier. The Library of Celsus, the terrace-house mosaics, a theatre that seated 24,000. Go early — the marble throws heat by midday.
  • Port
    Crete (Heraklion), GreeceKnossos is 15 minutes from the port and the Archaeological Museum in town holds everything they took out of it. Cretan food is its own thing — more greens, more herbs, more olive oil than seems reasonable.
  • Disembark
    Athens (Piraeus), GreeceStay on for Athens or connect home from ATH. If you have the days, the Acropolis deserves more than a layover.
