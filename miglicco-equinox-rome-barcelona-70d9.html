The Trip

One coastline, three countries, no backtracking

You board at Civitavecchia and step off nine nights later in Barcelona — a genuine one-way run along the top of the western Mediterranean rather than a loop that spends its last days sailing home.

The route works because it follows the coast in one direction. Naples first, for Pompeii and the best pizza in Italy. Then north to La Spezia, which puts Florence, Pisa and the Cinque Terre villages all within reach of a single day. Portofino is the one people don't expect — a pastel village on its own small peninsula that most large ships can't call at. Then across to the French Riviera, and finally two Spanish ports: Palma de Mallorca and Barcelona itself.

Two days at sea are built in, which on this ship is not a consolation. Equinox has a real lawn on the top deck — grass, not turf — and the AquaClass staterooms you're looking at come with their own restaurant and spa access.

We don't book hotels. We build trips. Custom guides, trip app, real human when something goes sideways. The work you'd hate doing — already done.

  • Embark
    Rome (Civitavecchia), ItalyThe port sits about 90 minutes northwest of central Rome. Come in ahead of the sailing — the Colosseum, the Vatican and a proper dinner in Trastevere are not a same-day proposition after a transatlantic flight.
  • Port
    Naples, ItalyPompeii is roughly 40 minutes out; Herculaneum is smaller, better preserved and far less crowded. The historic centre is a UNESCO site, and the Museo Archeologico Nazionale holds most of what was carried out of both excavations.
  • Port
    Florence / Pisa (La Spezia), ItalyOne port, three very different days. Florence is a little over two hours by rail, Pisa rather closer, and the five Cinque Terre villages start just minutes up the coast. Pick one properly rather than attempting all three.
  • Port
    Portofino, ItalyA pastel fishing village on its own small peninsula, with a harbour of yachts, the Church of St. Martin and Castello Brown on the hill above. The harbour is far too small for a ship this size to berth, so you'll anchor offshore and come in by tender — worth knowing in advance if anyone would rather not manage a tender boat.
  • Port
    The French RivieraThe Côte d'Azur day: palm-lined promenade, a covered market worth an hour on its own, and the Îles de Lérins a short ferry offshore, where Fort Royal supplied the Man in the Iron Mask legend.
  • Port
    Palma de Mallorca, SpainOften described as a smaller Barcelona — a Gothic cathedral above the water, Roman and Moorish layers underneath, and Es Baluard's modern art museum built into a 16th-century bastion.
  • Disembark
    Barcelona, SpainGaudí, the Gothic Quarter, and one of the great eating cities in Europe. Worth staying on rather than transferring straight to the airport — this is a city that rewards two or three days.

The second of Celebrity's five Solstice-class ships, launched in 2009 and taken through the line's full Celebrity Revolution refit in 2019 — new staterooms, new public rooms, new finishes throughout. She carries 2,852 guests across 14 decks — mid-sized by current standards, which is the useful size: enough to carry a full theatre, a spa and a genuine range of restaurants, small enough that you aren't queuing forty minutes to get off in port.

The top deck

Half an acre of real, growing grass — the Lawn Club, and the thing Solstice-class ships have always been known for. Lawn games, open-air concerts and the best place on the ship to watch a coastline go by. One note: Celebrity is rolling out a redesigned version of this deck, called Sunset Park, across the class. Equinox's turn hasn't been dated yet, so I'll confirm which version you'll sail with as we get closer.

Eating well

Tuscan Grille and Murano are the two specialty rooms worth booking. Craft Social handles beer and comfort food. The Martini Bar's flair bartenders are a performance in their own right, and the ice-topped bar is where the ship gathers before dinner.

The Spa

A full treatment menu plus the Persian Garden — a thermal suite of aromatic steam and heated stone loungers. AquaClass guests have access included, which is the quiet advantage of the category you're booked in.

Connected

Starlink high-speed internet across the ship. Also on board: the Sunset Bar at the stern, a casino, the theatre, and a Silent Disco that is far better than it sounds.

One thing worth clearing up before you look at the deck plan. Deck 11 is named the Penthouse deck on Celebrity Equinox — that's the name of the deck itself, because the ship's Penthouse Suites sit at one end of it. It does not mean these are penthouse staterooms. What you're being quoted is a Prime AquaClass veranda stateroom, category A1. If you see "Deck 11 Penthouse" on a deck plan, it's telling you the floor, not the room.

AquaClass is Celebrity's spa category, and it comes with two things the standard veranda rooms don't: your own restaurant and the thermal suite.

  • Blu — a restaurant reserved for AquaClass guests only. Lighter cooking, no queue, no assigned seating time, and small enough that the staff know you by the second night.
  • The Persian Garden — access to the spa's thermal suite included, which is otherwise a paid add-on
  • Private veranda with seating, and floor-to-ceiling glass
  • Deck 11 — an accommodation deck rather than a thoroughfare. The Library, two floors of quiet reading space, sits just aft of these staterooms; the Relaxation Lounge is at the forward end
  • Spa-inspired stateroom amenities and bath products
  • Twice-daily housekeeping and a dedicated attendant
